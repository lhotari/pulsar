# PIP-494: End-to-end produce backpressure

# Background knowledge

Publishing to Pulsar crosses three boundaries, and each can be overrun independently:

1. **Application → producer client.** The application hands messages to a `Producer`; the client holds them until the broker acknowledges them.
2. **Producer client → broker.** The client writes `CommandSend` frames onto a TCP connection shared with the client's other producers and consumers; the broker holds them until they are persisted.
3. **Broker → storage.** The broker writes entries to BookKeeper and holds them until the write completes.

"Backpressure" here means: when a downstream boundary cannot keep up, the pressure is transmitted upstream so the upstream party slows down, rather than accumulating unbounded work in the middle.

## What exists today

**At boundary 1**, a producer has two behaviours, selected by `blockIfQueueFull`:

- `false` (the default, `ProducerConfigurationData:87`): a send that exceeds available capacity **fails** with `ProducerQueueIsFullError` / `MemoryBufferIsFullError`.
- `true`: the send **parks the calling thread** until capacity exists.

What bounds that capacity is easy to get wrong. `maxPendingMessages` defaults to **`0` — no message-count limit at all** (`ProducerConfigurationData:55`); `PulsarClientImpl:819` substitutes 1000 only when the client memory limit is disabled. So on stock defaults, on **both** the v4 and V5 clients, the client memory limit is the sole bound on a producer's in-flight window.

That limit is `ClientBuilder.memoryLimit`, and it is documented as **a limit on direct memory** (`ClientBuilder:483`, default 64 MiB). It is charged in `ProducerImpl.canEnqueueRequest` in units of `uncompressedSize` — the payload, and only the payload, charged uncompressed even when the buffer retained is the compressed one.

**At boundary 2**, connections are shared: `connectionsPerBroker` defaults to `1` (`ClientConfigurationData:169`), so every producer and consumer a client owns for a broker multiplexes onto one TCP connection.

**Inside the broker**, `ServerCnxThrottleTracker.ThrottleType` enumerates seven throttling conditions:

| Condition | Blast radius | Publish path? | On by default? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `TopicPublishRate` | connection | yes | yes |
| `ResourceGroupPublishRate` | connection | yes | yes |
| `BrokerPublishRate` | connection | yes | yes |
| `IOThreadMaxPendingPublishBytesExceeded` | **every connection on the IO thread** | yes | yes |
| `ConnectionMaxPendingPublishRequestsExceeded` | connection | yes | yes |
| `ConnectionOutboundBufferFull` | connection | no — consumer dispatch | no |
| `ConnectionPauseReceivingCooldownRateLimit` | connection | no — all commands | no |

The last two fire from `channelWritabilityChanged` and from a check running for every command except PING/PONG, and both are gated on `pulsarChannelPauseReceivingRequestsIfUnwritable`, default `false` (`ServiceConfiguration:1076`). So a default broker has **five live publish-throttling conditions**.

**The only action any of them has** is `setAutoRead(false)` on a connection. `ServerCnxThrottleTracker.markThrottled` applies it on the transition into the throttled state. `IOThreadMaxPendingPublishBytesExceeded` is worse than connection-scoped: it iterates `cnxsPerThread` and throttles **every connection sharing that IO thread** (`ServerCnx:305-312`).

# Motivation

Pulsar does not lack limits. The problems are that they collapse into one blunt action applied at the wrong scope, that the client-side ones offer no usable third option, that heap retention is neither bounded nor accounted, and that on the newest client the bound does not engage at all.

### The lever is connection-scoped; three of the five live conditions are not

`TopicPublishRate`, `ResourceGroupPublishRate` and `BrokerPublishRate` describe a topic, a resource group and a broker. All are enforced by pausing a connection.

With `connectionsPerBroker = 1`, an application producing to twenty topics through one client has all twenty share a connection. One topic breaching its publish rate stops reads for the other nineteen and for every consumer on that client. A well-behaved tenant's latency is decided by the worst-behaved topic it shares a connection with. This is [#23403](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/23403); [PIP-385](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/pull/23398) documents the same problem.

### The mechanism cannot attribute cause, and its own metric shows it

`setAutoRead(false)` transmits no information: the client sees latency and cannot distinguish throttling from a slow network, a slow bookie, or a GC pause.

Metrics exist — a connection rate-limit counter, a throttled-connections gauge, and a per-topic publish-rate-limit counter (`OpenTelemetryTopicStats:69`). What is missing is attribution and duration. The per-topic counter is fed by `ServerCnx.increasePublishLimitedTimesForTopics()` (`ServerCnx:4471-4480`), which iterates **every producer on the connection** and increments the counter on **every topic it finds** — culprit and victims alike. The metric is systematically misattributed because the mechanism it reports on cannot tell them apart either, and the connection counter is not labelled by condition.

### The client offers no third option

`blockIfQueueFull` forces a choice between discarding work and parking a thread. Neither suits an asynchronous application: failing throws away work that could have been paced; parking defeats the point of an async API and can wedge a shared executor. There is no way to say *"tell me when you have room; I will decide what to do meanwhile."* This is [#26343](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/26343).

Parking has produced real deadlocks: a send issued from a client IO thread parks that thread on memory only that same thread can release ([#26344](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/26344)).

### Heap retention is neither bounded nor accounted

`memoryLimit` bounds direct memory. A pending unbatched send also retains **heap** — the `MessageMetadata`, the `MessageImpl`, the `OpSendMsg`, the send callback, and the chain of `CompletableFuture` stages carrying the result back — on the order of 1–2 KiB per pending send depending on message shape and JVM settings (measured on JDK 21 with ZGC, where compressed oops are off).

Nothing bounds that heap. Because the direct charge counts payload only, a producer of small messages exhausts heap long before it approaches its direct-memory limit. Measured on a heap dump of a client that died this way: the limiter read **92,335,360 of 209,715,200 bytes — 44% of its configured budget — when a 2 GiB heap was exhausted**, with 1,285,311 sends outstanding and **not one message acknowledged**. Even had every outstanding send been charged, the total would have been 164 MiB against a 200 MiB limit: the direct-memory limit could not have engaged, because direct memory was never the resource running out.

### On the V5 client there is no boundary-1 backpressure at all

`ScalableTopicProducer.sendInternalAsync` appends to a per-segment dispatch chain and returns; the limit is enforced later, on another thread. Appending always succeeds, so a caller that does not await the returned futures is never slowed. The V5 `ProducerBuilder` does expose `blockIfQueueFull(boolean)` — a knob naming a queue whose size cannot be set and whose blocking never engages. This is [#26470](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/26470).

## Goals

### In Scope

- A non-blocking way for an application to learn how much it may publish, and to be told when that changes.
- A bounded, accounted heap budget for pending sends, without redefining `memoryLimit`.
- Boundary-1 backpressure that works on the V5 client.
- Applying the **rate-limit** conditions to the producer that caused them, instead of to a connection.
- Making throttling attributable: which producer, which condition, how long.

### Out of Scope

- **Consumer-side flow control.** `CommandFlow` permits already exist for dispatch and are unchanged.
- **The memory-pressure conditions** (`IOThreadMaxPendingPublishBytesExceeded`, `ConnectionMaxPendingPublishRequestsExceeded`). Pausing remains correct there — see *Pacing does not apply to memory pressure*.
- **A fairness or weighted-allocation policy.** Analysed in *Fairness* and deliberately deferred; this proposal is its precondition, not its delivery.
- **Replacing the rate limiters.** `PublishRateLimiterImpl` and `ResourceGroupPublishLimiter` keep computing what they compute; this changes what happens when they trip.
- **A per-producer connection-isolation API**, tracked as #23403 and independently shippable.
- **A new binary protocol command.** See *Alternatives*.

# High Level Design

One organising idea: **capacity is expressed as a grant, never as a limit.** A grant answers "how much may I publish right now" — a number, valid now, possibly smaller than requested. It is never a published ceiling a caller can read and cache. That is what lets the source of capacity change over time without changing the interface the application uses.

```mermaid
flowchart TB
    App["Application"]
    subgraph Client["Producer client"]
      Permits["Capacity grants<br/>(messages + payload bytes)"]
      Heap["Pending-send heap budget"]
    end
    subgraph Broker["Broker"]
      Pace["Per-producer pacing<br/>(rate-limit conditions)"]
      Auto["Connection pause<br/>(memory pressure, and escalation)"]
    end
    App -- "1. acquire(request) → grant ≤ request" --> Permits
    Permits --- Heap
    Permits -- "CommandSend" --> Pace
    Pace -- "2. response stream, paced when over the limiter's rate" --> Permits
    Pace -. "3. when pacing is insufficient" .-> Auto
```

**Layer 1 — application to client: capacity grants.** The application asks for capacity for a range with a deadline and receives a grant in that range, or a timeout. A grant smaller than the maximum *is* the signal that capacity is short; no separate "near your limit" notification is proposed, because the grant carries it.

**Layer 1b — a heap budget.** A separate, explicitly named budget bounds the heap that pending sends retain. `memoryLimit` keeps its documented meaning.

**Layer 2 — broker to client: pacing the response stream.** When a rate limiter would throttle, the broker paces the responsible producer's responses instead of pausing its connection. The client's in-flight window drains more slowly, so that producer slows. No protocol change.

**Layer 3 — apply the limiter's decision per producer.** The existing limiters already decide *whether* to throttle. This proposal changes only *whom* that decision is applied to: the producer, rather than the connection it happens to be on. **No new allocation policy is introduced.**

Layer 2's control authority over a client is proportional to how tightly that client's in-flight window is bounded. On stock defaults that window is bounded only in direct memory and only by payload bytes, so it is very large. Layer 1b is what makes Layer 2 effective; #26470 is what makes it effective on V5.

# Detailed Design

## Design & Implementation Details

### Layer 1: capacity grants on the client

```java
/** Reached from the producer: {@code producer.capacity()}. */
public interface ProducerCapacity {
    CompletableFuture<CapacityGrant> acquire(CapacityRequest request);
}

/** Value object so the request can gain dimensions without an overload explosion. */
public final class CapacityRequest {
    public static Builder builder();
    public interface Builder {
        Builder minMessages(int min);       // default 1
        Builder maxMessages(int max);       // required
        Builder maxPayloadBytes(long max);  // default: unbounded
        Builder timeout(Duration timeout);  // required
        CapacityRequest build();
    }
}

public interface CapacityGrant {
    int messages();
    /** Application payload bytes this grant covers. */
    long payloadBytes();
    /** Return the unused remainder. Idempotent. */
    void releaseUnused();
}
```

**A grant is consumed by passing it to a send.** This is the part an implementer cannot infer:

```java
CapacityGrant grant = producer.capacity().acquire(request).get();
producer.newMessage(grant).value(payload).sendAsync();
```

Implicit "the next sends from this producer consume the grant" accounting is not specifiable under concurrency — two callers holding two grants on one producer cannot be told apart, and one would consume the other's reservation. Explicit ownership is the only unambiguous option. The rules:

- A send carrying a grant debits that grant, and debits nothing else. It does not re-enter the ordinary admission path, so it cannot be double-charged or blocked on capacity it already holds.
- A send carrying a grant with insufficient remaining capacity fails with `IllegalArgumentException`; it does not silently fall back.
- A grant presented to a different producer fails with `IllegalArgumentException`.
- Capacity a grant covers is released as its sends complete; whatever is left is released by `releaseUnused()`.
- `newMessage()` without a grant behaves exactly as today.

Why the rest is shaped this way:

- **Partial grants.** Asking for 1..1000 and receiving 40 tells the application the current capacity without publishing a limit it might cache.
- **A minimum.** An application batching 100 cannot act on a grant of 3. Without `min` it must busy-retry, and busy-retry against a bounded deferral queue manufactures queue-full errors under exactly the load the feature exists for.
- **A deadline that returns control.** This is the third mode #26343 asks for, and the one thing neither `blockIfQueueFull` setting provides. On expiry: `PulsarClientException.CapacityTimeoutException`. If the deferral queue is full: `PulsarClientException.CapacityQueueFullException`, immediately.
- **A request object, not positional arguments.** `acquire(int, int, long, Duration)` is three adjacent numbers with no room to add `minPayloadBytes` later without overloads. The value object is additive forever.
- **`releaseUnused()`, not `AutoCloseable`.** `AutoCloseable` invites `try (grant) { …sendAsync(); }`, where the block exits long before the sends complete — advertising a lifetime the design does not have. It must be **idempotent**: [#26469](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/26469) is an in-tree double-release that permanently disables the client memory limit, exactly what an unspecified release would reproduce.
- **`payloadBytes()` is application-facing payload**, never payload-plus-overhead. An application asking for 1 MiB means a megabyte of its own data; anything else makes its arithmetic silently wrong, and wrong by a different factor whenever client internals change.

**Interaction rule.** When `minMessages` and `maxPayloadBytes` are jointly unsatisfiable, the request waits until both can be met and times out if they cannot. It never returns fewer than `minMessages`, and never a byte budget that cannot carry them at the producer's observed average message size.

**Relationship to `AsyncSemaphore`.** This *builds on* `org.apache.pulsar.common.semaphore.AsyncSemaphore` and `AsyncDualMemoryLimiter` — in `pulsar-common`, used by `TopicListService`, `ServerCnx` and `LookupProxyHandler`, and on the dependency path of both `pulsar-client` and `pulsar-client-v5`. It is not a drop-in use of them. Three extensions are part of this proposal's work:

1. **Per-call timeout.** Today the timeout is fixed per instance (`AsyncSemaphoreImpl:79`), not per `acquire`.
2. **Partial grant.** `acquire(permits)` is all-or-nothing (`AsyncSemaphore:38`); `min..max` has no expression.
3. **Multi-dimensional acquire.** `AsyncDualMemoryLimiter` acquires **one category at a time** (`AsyncDualMemoryLimiter:45`). Messages, payload bytes and heap must be acquired atomically, or in a specified order with a specified rollback — otherwise the deadlock is left for the implementer to invent.

The deferral queue must be configured with a **positive** bound: `AsyncSemaphoreImpl:83` falls back to an unbounded `LinkedBlockingQueue` when the size is non-positive, so "the queue is bounded" is a thing this proposal must specify, not a property inherited by choosing the class.

**Existing behaviour is unchanged.** `blockIfQueueFull` keeps both meanings; `acquire` is an additional way to interact with the same capacity.

### Layer 1b: a heap budget, not a redefinition

`ClientBuilder.memoryLimit` is documented as a limit on **direct memory**. Charging retained heap against it would change the resource being limited, not merely improve its accuracy — a silent, incompatible redefinition of a public knob, which is not acceptable by default in a minor release (`CODING.md:219`).

Instead: **`memoryLimit` is unchanged**, and a separate `pendingSendHeapLimit` bounds the heap that pending sends retain. `AsyncDualMemoryLimiter` already models `HEAP_MEMORY` and `DIRECT_MEMORY` as distinct categories; this gives the heap category the bound it never had.

The default is chosen so a client whose messages are large enough for direct memory to bind first sees no change; small-message producers — the ones that exhaust heap today — are bounded where they previously were not. That is a behaviour change for exactly the workloads that are currently unsafe, and it is a *new* limit rather than a redefinition of an old one, so it is visible in configuration and independently disable-able.

The overhead figure is a range, not a constant, and depends on message shape (properties, ordering key, schema version) and on batching, which amortises the per-`OpSendMsg` cost but not the per-message retention inside the batch container. An implementation must state its accounting model and avoid double-charging buffers already charged to the direct budget.

### Layer 2: pacing the response stream

When a rate limiter would throttle, the broker paces the responsible producer's responses rather than pausing its connection.

**What is paced is an ordered per-producer response stream, not an isolated receipt.** This is the invariant an implementer most needs. `SendReceipt` and `SendError` travel the same stream, and `ProducerImpl.ackReceived` (`ProducerImpl:1362-1371`) **closes the connection** when it sees a sequence id greater than the head of its pending queue:

```java
if (sequenceId > op.sequenceId) {
    // Force connection closing so that messages can be re-transmitted in a new connection
    cnx.channel().close();
```

**Per producer, responses of every kind must be delivered in non-decreasing sequence-id order.** Holding receipt N while releasing receipt N+1 — or an *error* for N+1 — does not throttle a producer, it drops its connection. An implementation paces a queue, not individual messages. Geo-replication responses carry `(replSequenceLId, replSequenceEId)` rather than a producer sequence id, so the ordering key differs for replicator producers.

**Why pacing reaches the application.** The client releases its own capacity on receipt — `ackReceived` calls `releaseSemaphoreForSendOp` (`ProducerImpl:1389`). Pacing is therefore spent out of the client's Layer-1 budget: holding a producer at rate `r` for delay `D` forces it to retain roughly `r · D` messages' worth, and when that exceeds its budget the application receives a partial grant or a timeout. That is the backpressure arriving where a decision can be made about it.

**What pacing is not.** It is best-effort, not a rate controller:

- The broker does not know the client's configured window. `CommandProducer` has no window field; observed outstanding messages reflect application demand, batching and storage latency as much as configuration.
- Little's law is an approximation, not an allocation algorithm: for a saturated fixed window, `r ≈ W / (R₀ + D)`. Realising a target rate requires assuming `W` and the baseline latency `R₀`. A finite delay bound cannot realise arbitrarily small rates for large windows, and below saturation added delay may not reduce the sending rate at all.
- Batching breaks the message-denominated form: one `OpSendMsg` is one window slot and up to `batchingMaxMessages` (default 1000) messages, a ratio the client chooses and the broker cannot observe. **The control variable is bytes**, which the broker measures directly; message-rate conditions are influenced only indirectly.
- **Per-producer pacing does not give per-producer isolation on the client.** A paced producer's sends keep occupying the client-wide memory budget (`ProducerImpl:1130`), so it can still prevent unrelated producers on the same client from enqueueing — even though its connection is being read. Pacing removes the *broker-side* head-of-line blocking, not the client-side shared budget.

**Clients may advertise their window, with no protocol change.** `CommandProducer.metadata` is an existing `repeated KeyValue` (field 6, `PulsarApi.proto:516`). A client may publish its pending-message limit, memory limit and `sendTimeoutMs` there, letting the broker pace precisely rather than conservatively. The hint is **advisory and untrusted**: the broker clamps any advertised value to a configured maximum and continues to enforce its own accounting. It may make pacing more precise; it must never grant more capacity.

**A bound is required, and it bounds the right quantity.** `producerResponsePacingMaxAgeMillis` bounds **the age of the oldest undelivered response for a producer**, not the per-response increment. At saturation head-of-queue age is `window / rate` regardless of the increment: a 50 ms increment with 1000 in flight is 50 seconds of head age. Beyond the bound the broker escalates to a connection pause.

The bound exists because `sendTimeoutMs` (default 30 s) is a **cliff, not a slope**: `ProducerImpl.run(Timeout)` measures from the first pending message's creation and on expiry calls `failPendingMessages`, failing the producer's **entire in-flight window**. Pacing slightly too hard would discard a whole window and have it retry into a broker already short of capacity — worse than the pause it replaces. The bound reduces that risk; it **cannot eliminate it**, because a message may already have spent most of its timeout in batching, transit or persistence before pacing adds anything.

#### Pacing does not apply to memory pressure

For `IOThreadMaxPendingPublishBytesExceeded` and `ConnectionMaxPendingPublishRequestsExceeded` the broker is **already over budget when the condition fires**. Pacing relieves nothing for a full round trip, and if it also defers completion bookkeeping the broker's pending counters stay inflated for the duration — pacing then sustains the condition that triggered it. A memory watermark is a guard, not a scheduling preference.

For those conditions **pausing remains the primary and correct instrument**, unchanged. PIP-385 reached the same conclusion. This scope cut is what makes the rest of Layer 2 defensible.

#### Consequences that must be stated

- **The duplicate window widens.** `brokerDeduplicationEnabled` defaults to `false` (`ServiceConfiguration:988`). Today the gap between "persisted" and "receipt delivered" is one scheduling hop; pacing widens it deliberately, on the busiest producers. On reconnect the client replays its pending queue, duplicating those messages on the topic. Operators who cannot tolerate this must enable deduplication; the pacing bound also bounds this exposure.
- **Transactional producers are exempt.** A commit awaits every send future and `TransactionImpl` times out independently (default 60 s); accumulated pacing could abort committed-intent work.
- **Chunked messages are paced as a unit**, since a chunked message occupies `totalChunks` window slots and its callback fires only on the last chunk.
- **On producer close or topic unload the pacing queue is flushed, not discarded.** A discarded response is a guaranteed client-side timeout cliff.

### Layer 3: applying the decision to the producer

The existing limiters already decide *whether* to throttle. `PublishRateLimiterImpl.handlePublishThrottling` already tracks per-producer state and drains an unthrottling queue. This proposal changes only *whom* the decision is applied to.

For `TopicPublishRate`, `ResourceGroupPublishRate` and `BrokerPublishRate`, the producers that contributed to the breach are paced in proportion to their contribution, rather than the connection of whichever producer tripped the limit being paused. **No weights, no demand estimation, no new entitlement.** The aggregate rate the limiter enforces is unchanged; only the distribution of the resulting slowdown changes, from "whoever shares a connection with the culprit" to "the producers actually publishing".

`producerFairShareEnabled` gates this. It is a safety guard with its escape valve: Layer 3 changes who gets served on a loaded broker, with no wire signal and no client-visible error, and there is no in-tree precedent for a paced response stream. An operator meeting this at 03:00 needs a config flip, not a rollback.

### Fairness

The user-visible goal beyond overload avoidance is that a loaded broker share capacity predictably rather than by arrival order. This proposal is the **precondition** for that and deliberately does not deliver it: you cannot allocate fairly until you can throttle a producer at all, which today you cannot.

What a fairness policy would additionally need to specify, none of which this proposal settles:

- **The allocation identity.** Sharing a topic's rate equally *between producers* gives a topic with 100 producers a hundred times the capacity of a topic with one; sharing a resource group's rate between producers rewards producer proliferation. Producer count is not an entitlement, and authentication does not make it one. The identity must be stated per condition and is a behaviour contract that cannot be changed after ship.
- **Weights and their source** — configuration, resource group, tenant.
- **What "active demand" means**, given the broker observes offered load, not desire.
- **Redistribution cadence, starvation bounds, and computational cost** at thousands of producers per broker.
- **A threat model.** A client can manipulate what an estimator observes: create producers, change batching, alternate idle and burst phases, reconnect (does throttle debt reset?), or let sends time out to release local capacity and send again.

Until those are answered, a "fair share" claim would be unearned. A follow-up PIP can consume this proposal's per-producer pacing mechanism.

**AIMD is not proposed** for the broker side. Additive-increase/multiplicative-decrease converges to fairness precisely because participants cannot coordinate and must infer congestion from a shared signal — the setting Chiu and Jain analysed. A broker has complete knowledge of its producers and can allocate directly. AIMD is the right tool for a *client* inferring capacity unaided; it is the wrong tool for an allocator that already knows the answer.

### Connection sharing

Connection sharing is why a topic-scoped limit becomes a client-wide outage today, and it is why this proposal exists. The fix proposed here is to stop using a connection-scoped instrument for conditions that are not connection-scoped — not to give every producer its own connection.

`connectionsPerBroker` is client-wide: raising it spreads everything across more connections and cannot isolate one producer. A per-producer isolation API would address the residue and is tracked as [#23403](https://github.com/apache/pulsar/issues/23403). It is **independently shippable and not specified here**, for two reasons: with Layers 2 and 3 in place, most of what isolation is for stops existing, and the residual cannot be sized until then; and specifying it first would make it the workaround people build on.

Isolation also cannot deliver what it appears to. `IOThreadMaxPendingPublishBytesExceeded` throttles every connection sharing an IO thread (`ServerCnx:305-312`), and a paced producer still occupies the client-wide memory budget. A dedicated socket isolates a socket, not client memory, an IO thread, or storage.

## Public-facing Changes

### Public API

- New: `ProducerCapacity`, `CapacityRequest`, `CapacityGrant`, reached via `producer.capacity()`; `newMessage(CapacityGrant)` on both the v4 and V5 producer APIs.
- New: `PulsarClientException.CapacityTimeoutException`, `PulsarClientException.CapacityQueueFullException`.
- No existing method changes signature or semantics. `memoryLimit` keeps its documented meaning.

### Binary protocol

**None.** Layer 2 paces existing responses; the optional client hint uses the existing `CommandProducer.metadata` field.

### Configuration

Client:

- `pendingSendHeapLimit` — bound on heap retained by pending sends. New limit, not a redefinition.

Broker:

- `producerResponsePacingMaxAgeMillis` — bound on the age of a producer's oldest undelivered response before escalating to a connection pause.
- `producerFairShareEnabled` — whether rate-limit decisions are applied per producer or, as today, by pausing the connection.
- `producerAdvertisedWindowMaxMessages` / `...MaxBytes` — clamps on the untrusted `CommandProducer.metadata` hint.

### Metrics

Named in the current OTel convention, not the deprecated `pulsar_*` snake-case style — a metric rename inside five years breaks every dashboard built on it.

- `pulsar.broker.producer.throttled.duration` (histogram; attributes: topic, `reason` ∈ the three rate-limit conditions) — **elapsed time per producer throttling episode**, from first pacing to resumption, not per response. Topic cardinality is bounded by the existing topic-level metric configuration. When conditions overlap, the episode is attributed to the condition that began it.
- `pulsar.broker.producer.throttle.escalation.count` (counter; attributes: topic, reason) — how often pacing was insufficient and the connection was paused.

# Monitoring

The operator's question is *"is anything being throttled, why, and is it getting worse."* `pulsar.broker.producer.throttled.duration` answers all three: non-zero means throttling, `reason` says which condition binds, and the trend says whether capacity is being outgrown. This is the attribution `increasePublishLimitedTimesForTopics` cannot provide.

`pulsar.broker.producer.throttle.escalation.count` should be near zero in a healthy cluster. Sustained escalation means pacing is not containing load and connections are being paused — the noisy-neighbour condition this proposal exists to remove. Alert on it.

# Testing

The failure modes are timing-dependent connection closes and whole-window send failures, so a clean local run is weak evidence. The proposal is not implementable without at least:

- A paced producer's responses remain non-decreasing in sequence id under interleaved `SendError`, and the connection is not closed — the invariant that fails as a connection drop rather than a wrong value.
- Pacing a producer to its bound surfaces at the application as a partial grant or timeout, not a send timeout — the budget-coupling claim.
- A producer paced to the configured age bound escalates to a pause rather than exceeding it.
- Transactional producers are not paced; a chunked message is paced as a unit.
- Pacing one producer does not delay another producer's sends on the same connection — the property that justifies the design.
- A grant is debited exactly once; `releaseUnused()` twice is a no-op; a grant presented to the wrong producer is rejected.
- Coverage for small `sendTimeout`, batching, disabled limits, multiple producers per connection, and connection churn.

# Security Considerations

Throttling decisions are per producer, and a producer is already authenticated and authorised for its topic. Because Layer 3 introduces **no new entitlement** — it only redistributes a slowdown the limiter already decided on — it does not create a way for one tenant to obtain more capacity than today. It does change *who* absorbs a given limiter decision: today an unrelated tenant sharing a connection absorbs part of it, and after this change the producers actually publishing do.

That is not a fairness guarantee, and this proposal does not claim one. In particular, a tenant running many producers already obtains more of a shared rate than a tenant running one, both before and after this change. Establishing an entitlement is the subject of the deferred fairness work, and it is the reason that work needs a threat model rather than an algorithm alone.

The `CommandProducer.metadata` hint is advisory and untrusted: it is clamped to a configured maximum and never grants capacity beyond the limiter's decision.

Bounding the pacing age matters for availability: an unbounded delay would let a broker silently hold a client's sends until they time out. Bounding it and escalating visibly keeps the failure mode observable.

No new endpoints, commands or credentials are introduced.

# Backward & Forward Compatibility

## Upgrade

Layers 2 and 3 are broker-side and need no client change; `producerFairShareEnabled` disables them. Layer 1 is additive client-side API.

`pendingSendHeapLimit` is a **new** limit, so nothing silently changes meaning. It does bind on workloads that were previously unbounded in heap — which is the point — so it ships disabled-by-default for one release, with the computed value exposed as a metric so operators can see where it would bind on their own traffic, and enabled by default in the following release.

Other changes worth stating: a paced producer sees higher send-completion latency and greater timeout exposure; client memory occupancy rises for the duration of pacing; and lower admitted capacity surfaces as earlier queue-full errors or longer waits, not only as lower throughput.

## Downgrade / Rollback

Reverting the broker restores connection-scoped pausing; reverting the client restores the previous accounting. No persisted state and no wire format changes, so rollback is unconditional. A downgraded broker ignores any `CommandProducer.metadata` hint.

## Pulsar Geo-Replication Upgrade & Downgrade/Rollback Considerations

Replicator producers publish through the same path and are paced by the same mechanism — this proposal does not add geo-replication-specific throttling, which remains out of scope. Two specifics: replicator responses carry replication sequence pairs rather than a producer sequence id, so the pacing queue's ordering key differs; and the replicator disables its send timeout and sets a pending-message limit, which makes the timeout cliff inapplicable to it. Nothing crosses the replication wire format, so mixed-version clusters are unaffected.

# Alternatives

**An explicit throttling command in the protocol (PIP-385).** The strongest argument for it is that **acknowledging completed work and authorising future work are different responsibilities.** A paced receipt makes a client retain a message the broker already knows is persisted, consuming client memory and spending the message's timeout budget to communicate a different fact: slow down. An explicit command says that directly; it can reach a producer before its next burst, name the cause, and let the client apply its own timeout policy — none of which pacing can do. PIP-385 also supports incremental deployment through feature negotiation with a connection-pausing fallback, so "every language must implement it first" overstates its cost.

The honest accounting is that "no protocol change" is not free: it buys avoiding a visible protocol addition at the price of an implicit timing contract, window estimation, a response queue with ordering rules, and timeout interactions — and adding an explicit command later would leave three mechanisms to maintain rather than two.

This proposal still chooses pacing, for a narrower reason than "it is simpler": the gap that hurts applications today is at boundary 1, which no protocol command addresses, and pacing is the largest broker-side improvement obtainable without blocking on a protocol change and its client rollout. The two are complementary — an explicit command can be added over the same per-producer mechanism, and the `CommandProducer.metadata` hint is a strictly smaller step in that direction. What this proposal does **not** claim is that pacing delivers equivalent control to an explicit command.

**Pace a producer that is not currently sending.** Pacing can only slow a producer awaiting responses. A previously idle producer's first window arrives unthrottled. PIP-385's command does not have this limitation, which is the strongest argument in its favour.

**Keep connection pausing and add connection isolation only.** Isolation would reduce noisy neighbours but not overload, attribution or fairness, and it cannot isolate client memory or an IO thread.

**Redefine `memoryLimit` to cover heap.** Rejected: it is documented as a direct-memory limit, so this would silently change the resource being limited, contrary to `CODING.md:219`. A separate, explicitly named budget achieves the same protection compatibly.

**A blocking permit acquire.** Already achievable with `blockIfQueueFull(true)`. Rejected because it does not give the application control, and parking threads on the client's own executors has produced deadlocks (#26344).

# Design decisions

Recorded so a later pass does not re-litigate them.

**Cut — a fairness / weighted-allocation policy.** Its allocation identity, weights, demand definition and threat model are all unsettled, and shipping a "fair share" claim without them would be unearned. *Add-back:* a follow-up PIP consuming this proposal's per-producer pacing. *Tradeoff:* this PIP promises usable backpressure and correct attribution, not predictable allocation.

**Cut — redefining `memoryLimit`.** See *Alternatives*. *Add-back:* none needed; the separate heap budget is the durable answer. *Tradeoff:* two limits instead of one, in exchange for neither of them lying.

**Cut — a `pendingSendOverheadBytes` tuning knob.** The overhead is message-shape dependent and split across heap and direct pools, so no single global scalar is right and nothing produces the number an operator would type. *Add-back:* purely additive later. *Tradeoff:* deployments tune `pendingSendHeapLimit`, which is the quantity they actually care about.

**Cut — a client-side `granted / requested` ratio metric.** It is undefined for the case that matters — a timeout produces no grant, so the distribution omits the worst outcomes and reads *better* under starvation than under mild pressure — and a caller requesting `min=max=1` can wait seconds and still report 1. *Add-back:* client-side and additive. *Tradeoff:* applications instrument their own call site, where they also know what they did about it.

**Cut — the per-producer connection isolation API and named connection groups.** See *Connection sharing*. *Add-back:* a `ProducerBuilder` option plus a connection-pool key; nothing here forecloses it. *Tradeoff:* #23403 waits until the residue it addresses can be measured.

**Keep — `producerFairShareEnabled`.** A safety guard with its escape valve, for the highest-blast-radius change in the proposal.

**Keep — `minMessages`, both dimensions, and the explicit grant/send binding.** Each carries a real interaction rule with a real consumer; see Layer 1.

**Keep — the AIMD discussion.** It is the first question the design will be asked, and the answer is load-bearing.
